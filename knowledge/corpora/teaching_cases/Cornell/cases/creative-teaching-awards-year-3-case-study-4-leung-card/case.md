# Using Art to Build Community and Nurture Relationships in the Patient-Physician Dynamic

Learners engaged with patients outside of the clinical setting by participating in guided experiences at a local art museum, a mutually beneficial experience that helped build empathy and trust in the doctor-patient relationship.

## **Brief Summary**

Medical resident-patient pairs engaged in innovative museum-based learning experiences at a local art museum, as an exercise in social prescribing for psychosocial stressors that are often inadequately addressed in time-limited primary care visits. By creating a space where both patients and residents were on equal footing, relationships formed beyond the walls of the clinic, helping instill a broader sense of community and humanity for all participants. After participating in these experiences, medical residents consistently report transformative shifts in how they perceive their patients, moving from problem-focused clinical thinking to holistic appreciation of patients’ creativity, resilience, and inner lives.

## Context

Since 2017, this program, implemented within the Internal Medicine Residency Program at New York-Presbyterian/Weill Cornell Medicine, has engaged over 60 resident-patient pairs in museum-based learning experiences at the The Isamu Noguchi Foundation and Garden Museum in Long Island City, NY. The project was initially conceived by a primary care resident who wanted to explore social prescribing for patients—an approach in which clinicians recommend community-based activities, often involving art and music, as a way to intervene in social determinants of health, such as loneliness, isolation, and stress.

Drawing on constructivist and experiential learning theories, medical residents invited patients to the Noguchi Museum, where they participated in a 90-minute, museum-curated experience. Patients were recruited from the federally qualified health center, specifically those experiencing psychosocial stressors such as social isolation, financial burdens, and interpersonal conflict. Many of these patients were local to the museum’s neighborhood but had never had the opportunity to explore it. While the original intention was to help patients with psychosocial stressors, as time went on, Drs. Leung and Card noticed additional benefits to the medical residents themselves, both in increasing empathy and changing how they viewed their patients as collaborators in creating plans for care. This shaped future iterations of the program.

> “Giving the residents and their patients an experience outside the clinic walls is a reminder… that you’re still meeting a human being.”—Dr. Andrea Card

## Implementation

![image](https://blogs.cornell.edu/teachingcasestudies/files/2026/03/Students_Drawing-1024x768.jpg)Drawings made in response to reflective prompts during a personal response tour led by Noguchi Educator Holly Hepp-Galván. Photo provided by Queena Ko.

Before each visit, residents explored theoretical frameworks of social prescribing, community-academic partnerships, and the therapeutic potential of arts engagement. Residents identified patients under their care who were also members of the local community, and Drs. Leung and Card undertook the logistical planning to find a mutual time when the residents and patients could meet as a dyad, or pair, to visit the museum.

During each visit, the resident-patient pairs were guided by professional museum educators in art-viewing, sketching, and object-handling activities designed to promote self-reflection and dialogue. Using a published research protocol, participants responded to prompts that encouraged personal interpretation rather than “correct” answers.

After each visit, the residents were asked to reflect on the experience in didactic sessions that were part of their residency training. From these sessions, many residents identified community resources to incorporate into their patients’ longitudinal care plans—such as visits to the museum, local arts programs, and other cultural activities—as part of their patients’ social prescriptions.

> ” It makes patients seem more like “real” rather than clinical exercises. It highlights the fact that I can take time to get to know them during their visit in a way I have not done previously, like ask about hobbies or social activities, what they do for fun, etc.”—Internal Medicine Resident

> “When I listen to myself talk [sharing thoughts], I realize that I know some things and am smart. I never thought I was smart before. I am starting to come out of my shell. I am learning more about myself. I am feeling so relaxed. Coming out [of my shell] has made me so relaxed. I am so happy. I am so happy.”—Patient

## Challenges

Scheduling proved to be the largest challenge when coordinating the trips to the museum, due to the conflicting responsibilities of all involved parties. To help mitigate this, the instructors helped coordinate direct transportation for patients and offered a variety of times to accommodate schedules.

It was also vital that the instructors maintained a strong relationship with the Noguchi Museum educational team, as the team was crucial in the success of the program, creating guided tours for the patient-resident dyad, and helping with visit logistics. Mutual respect and shared vision helped drive the project and ensured the continuation of the experiences.

> “Intern year is obviously difficult, and we are not always given the space to stop and reflect on how an experience has truly affected us. The museum created that space for us.”—Internal Medicine Resident

## Reflection and Future Directions

![image](https://blogs.cornell.edu/teachingcasestudies/files/2026/03/Drawing_2.jpg)Artwork created for the internal medicine residents during the COVID-19 pandemic, by teen participants of The Noguchi Museum’s “Making Your Mark” program. Photos by Shannon Murphy, provided by Peggy Leung.

Over the course of the project, the faculty have seen the museum-based experience reshape how residents understand their patients and themselves as physicians, with many participants sharing that they find the encounters very meaningful and emotionally resonant. They noted that the dyadic setting deepens trust, accelerates the development of therapeutic relationships, and fosters empathy by allowing residents and patients to see one another as “real human beings” outside the constraints of clinical roles. Dr. Leung noted that it was a new and sometimes uncomfortable experience for some residents, as they do not often have time to pause and reflect during their education, as “so much of medicine… is go, go, go… you don’t get a chance to pause and think about ‘what actually sustains me?’ in medicine.”

Looking ahead, the faculty’s primary focus involves adapting the program following their clinic’s relocation from Long Island City to Manhattan and determining whether shuttling patients back to the Noguchi Museum remains feasible. They also plan to continue refining logistical supports, offering additional scheduling flexibility, and maintaining strong relationships with the museum educators whose expertise has been essential to the success of the program. Above all, they remain committed to sustaining an experience that allows both residents and patients to feel heard and seen outside of the traditional clinic walls.

> “The power dynamic is inherent in the patient-physician dyad, so when the museum guide asks the group what something means and the patients can offer their thoughts on par with their doctors, there’s something of a leveling that occurs. We are all just human beings trying to make meaning of the world around us. The skill here is listening mindfully and noticing different aspects of what our patients have to say.”
>
> —Internal Medicine Resident

## How to Adapt This Approach

Instructors interested in adopting a similar relationship-centered model can draw on several key practices that emerged from this program’s success. These strategies help ensure that community-embedded experiences remain grounded in pedagogical purpose, foster mutual respect, and support meaningful learning for all participants.

- **Start with authentic partnerships.**Identify community or cultural organizations invested in education and collaboration, who understand both pedagogical goals and needs of the target audience.
- **Equalize Roles & Location.** When working with patients, clients, or members of the community, the power lies in equalizing the relationship between both parties. This can be accomplished by sharing experiences with art or music, or another neutral medium. Focus on the medium and shared humanity of the experience.
- **Use constructivist approaches.** Avoid the urge to provide “correct” interpretations, as the learning emerges from the personal meaning-making and the dialogue surrounding it.
- **Think longitudinally**. Frame the activities or experiences as just one touchpoint in ongoing relationships, rather than an isolated intervention. Work to promote the creation of a relationship in which each participant can see the needs of the other and work mutually to ensure those needs are met (e.g., the residents identifying sustainable community resources for their patients).
