# Awe Walks and Sensory Savoring Routes: Ithaca’s Natural Landscapes as a Living Laboratory for a Course in Psychological Well-Being

Students engaged in structured, place-based experiences across Cornell’s campus and the Ithaca community to embody psychological concepts such as awe, savoring, flow, and social connection.

## **Brief Summary**

In Anthony Ong’s “The Science of Well‑Being” course, students completed four place‑based experiential activities—focused on savoring, community connection, flow, and awe—to engage directly with concepts introduced in readings and lectures. Each activity guided them through structured observation on campus and in community settings, followed by paired discussions and weekly reflections. Ong adopted these place‑based tasks after finding that previous at‑home assignments produced surface‑level insights; since implementing the new activities, student reflections have become significantly deeper and more self‑aware.

## Context

For the past 20 years, students in Anthony Ong’s “Science of Well-Being” course have studied research and theory from positive psychology and applied it to their lives through structured activities and data collection. Over time, the course has evolved from a traditional lecture course with abstract exercises to one centered on personal experience, peer discussion, and reflection. In recent years, Ong had grown increasingly frustrated with the lack of depth in students’ reflections. During the same period, students began asking for more experiential work, and Ong—aware of how strongly place and environment shape well‑being—recognized this was a missing dimension in the course. In Fall 2025, he introduced four place-based activities tied to specific concepts to address this gap. Students visited Cascadilla Gorge, Beebe Lake, and the Arts Quad on Cornell’s Ithaca campus, as well as Ithaca community organizations, using guided protocols that connected their embodied experiences to theoretical concepts.

![image](https://blogs.cornell.edu/teachingcasestudies/files/2026/03/2021_1215_SH_034-1024x683.jpg)Students visited Cascadilla Gorge as one of their place-based activities connecting embodied experiences to specific course concepts.

> “The experiential component… is by far the thing that students say was transformative for them.”—Anthony Ong

## Implementation

Each week, students learned about a well‑being concept in class and completed an activity to put the idea into practice. Activities took place at locations on campus or in the Ithaca community and used structured observation or conversation protocols to focus attention, reduce cognitive load, and elicit richer experiences.

### **Place-Based Activities**

![A moment of tranquility captured in the water cascading down the waterfall.](https://blogs.cornell.edu/teachingcasestudies/files/2026/03/pattern-1024x683.jpg)

#### **Gorge Awe Walk**

Students visited Cascadilla Gorge or Ithaca Falls and followed a structured observation protocol informed by research on awe. Before the visit, they read about how vast natural stimuli can shift self‑perception and increase prosocial behavior. On‑site, students completed timed attention exercises—such as observing water movement, tracking light patterns, and attending to layered sounds—designed to amplify awe responses. At left, Cascadilla Creek flows through Cascadilla Gorge, which cuts through Cornell’s Ithaca campus.

![Beebe Lake in the Fall](https://blogs.cornell.edu/teachingcasestudies/files/2026/03/Beebe_2update-1024x683.jpg)

#### ****Sensory Savoring Routes****

Students received prescribed campus routes tailored to weather and time of day (e.g., the Suspension Bridge at dusk, Arts Quad after snowfall, Beebe Lake at dawn). Each route included location‑specific prompts about what to attend to and what to let pass, grounded in research on savoring. The constraint of a predetermined route relieved cognitive load and supported deeper presence. At left, a photo of Beebe Lake in autumn.

![Rocking Climbing Wall at the Lindseth Climbing Center](https://blogs.cornell.edu/teachingcasestudies/files/2026/03/rock_climbing_update-1024x683.jpg)

#### ****Flow Mapping Experiences****

Using results from the VIA Character Strengths Survey, each student was assigned an on‑campus environment aligned with one of their signature strengths—for example, the ceramic studio for creativity, the climbing wall for bravery, or the Lab of Ornithology archives for curiosity. After participating in the experience, students documented the onset, maintenance, and dissolution of flow states within that specific environment. At left, a student scales the climbing wall at the Lindseth Climbing Center in Bartels Hall on Cornell’s Ithaca campus.

![Purchasing produce at the Ithaca Farmers Market](https://blogs.cornell.edu/teachingcasestudies/files/2026/03/Farmers_Market-1-1024x683.jpg)

#### ******Community Connection Hours******

Partnering with Ithaca organizations such as [Loaves and Fishes](https://loaves.org/), the [Ithaca Farmers Market](https://ithacamarket.com/) (photo at left), and [Southside Community Center](https://www.sspride.org/), students engaged in structured conversations with community members designed to build mutual trust and closeness. These interactions encouraged students to move beyond familiar peer networks and practice intentional, respectful engagement in community settings.

#### ********Course Integration********

- **Discussion Pods:** At the start of class, students discussed their experiences in fixed groups of two or three, sharing their observations for 15 minutes.
- **Weekly Reflection Papers**: Reflection assignments asked students to connect the place-based experiences to course readings and content.

> “I’m just now realizing I don’t actually look at things when I walk. Like ever. The light on the water wasn’t one thing, it kept shifting between yellow and silver depending on the angle, and I’ve had this ten minutes from my dorm for two years and never noticed.” —Student

> “I realized my conversations feel shallow, not because people are shallow, but because I keep them shallow. I give the polite response and keep walking, and then wonder why I feel disconnected. That’s on me.” — Student

## Challenges

Weather has proved to be the most impactful challenge for the project, given that Ithaca’s conditions can shift quickly, disrupting outdoor plans. Accessibility has also posed a potential challenge, especially for off-campus activities requiring transportation or for students who cannot complete longer walks. To address both concerns, Ong plans to continue refining his choice of locations to include more accessible options, and to create alternatives that maintain the course’s core ideas without requiring extensive mobility.

Another challenge that has arisen, given the nature of what is being asked of the students, is how to assess their varied experiences and reflections. Ong shared that he is looking into the role that rubrics can play in assessing student work, and how he may be able to incorporate other media, beyond the written word, for reflection assignments.

![image](https://blogs.cornell.edu/teachingcasestudies/files/2026/03/campus_strom-1024x575.jpg)A view of Beebe Lake during a winter snow storm.

Lastly, coordinating schedules with community partners in order to build relationships can present a logistical, and by now familiar, challenge. Currently, finding a mutually beneficial time for local organizations to host student volunteers requires substantial lead time to coordinate. Ong continues to look into ways he can establish a semester-long collaboration with community partners to allow for more consistent scheduling and relationship building.

## Reflection and Future Directions

Ong shared that the Fall 2025 place‑based reflections were by far the richest he’s seen, with students frequently describing these experiences as transformative. For future iterations, he hopes to incorporate additional ways for students to document their experiences beyond the written word (e.g., photos, video, or audio notes). He also intends to find more accessible alternatives and consider expanding the number of place-based tasks, though he remains mindful of the increased logistical demands given the course’s growing enrollment.

> “After trying this for a semester, the place‑based reflections were by far the richest I’ve seen.” —Anthony Ong

> “Something happened around minute three where the boredom opened up and I started seeing patterns. I don’t think I was savoring the way Bryant describes, but I was paying a different kind of attention and it turned boring into genuinely interesting. Maybe savoring isn’t about positive experiences. Maybe it’s about the quality of attention itself.” —Student

## How to Adapt This Approach

- **Start with a single place-based activity,** rather than designing multiple experiences at once.
- **Prepare students explicitly for ambiguity**, emphasizing “You’re not looking for a right answer. You’re practicing attention.”
- **Introduce the place-theory connection before the experience,** so students understand the psychological mechanisms they are engaging and the context of those mechanisms within the course.
- **Provide accessible experiences or virtual alternatives** (e.g., photos or videos of locations) to fit the needs of all your students.
