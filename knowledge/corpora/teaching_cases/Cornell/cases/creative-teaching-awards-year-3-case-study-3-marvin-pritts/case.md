# From Classroom to Field: Students Drive Real‑World Farm Innovation

Through visits to working berry farms and related coursework, students explore diverse farming methods and apply their learning to create a business and marketing plan designed for novice berry growers in New York State.

## **Brief Summary**

Over the course of the semester, students study the science of berry farming and food production systems through coursework and visits to local farms. At the same time, they work in teams to create a comprehensive plan for a novice berry grower. This requires students to integrate their knowledge across a wide range of disciplines—from plant biology, soil science, and entomology to agricultural economics, local market considerations, and climate science. In the process, students must take the perspective of the grower, which exposes them to the human and family dynamics that shape farm decisions—unique aspects of agriculture they would not ordinarily see in their coursework.

## Context

When Marvin Pritts first started teaching Berry Crops: Production and Management*,* he taught the way he thought he was supposed to teach: delivering lots of information and inundating students with charts and graphs. As he gained experience, he became increasingly interested in helping students apply what they were learning in authentic, real‑world contexts.

Through his [Cornell Cooperative Extension](https://cals.cornell.edu/cornell-cooperative-extension) appointment, Pritts frequently visited farms and worked directly with growers to diagnose production issues. The integrative, hands-on nature of this work—requiring him to synthesize plant health, soils, pests, climate, and economics—led him to consider how similar applied experiences could benefit his students.

Pritts’ first attempt at creating this type of experience for his class involved having student teams design a plan for a hypothetical berry farm in New York State. Students evaluated soils, identified what they would need to establish a farm, estimated costs, and integrated what they were learning in class into a comprehensive plan. The turning point came when one student used her team’s project to develop an actual working farm. That experience made Pritts realize there was no reason students couldn’t work with real growers from the start.

> “I’ve repeated this exercise for many years because it’s a win-win for all of us.”
>
> —Marvin Pritts

Because of his extension appointment, Pritts had access to a statewide network of extension educators who work directly with farms and farmers. They helped him connect with early‑stage berry growers—people who were just getting started, open to new ideas, and eager for guidance. These educators continue to help Pritts identify a suitable partner grower each semester the class is offered.

## Implementation

During the fall semester, before being paired with that semester’s partner farm, Pritts’ students visit five berry farms in central NY, ranging from small, pick-your-own operations to a 32-acre greenhouse that grows strawberries in winter for the wholesale market. Because many of the partner farms are located several hours away, the class is usually not able to visit the location they’ll create comprehensive plans for. Instead, the class field trips expose students to a range of production systems and give them concrete examples to draw on as they develop their plans.

To identify growers as potential partners for students, Pritts works closely with Cooperative Extension educators across New York State. Because these educators work directly with farms in their regions, they help him locate early-stage growers who are just beginning to consider berry production and may be more open to possibilities and benefit most from student input.

Pritts divides students into teams of four to six, typically including at least one graduate student or senior, one student with prior agricultural experience, and a plant or agricultural science major, to ensure that each team has complementary strengths. Some team members focus on production issues, such as proposing new crops or addressing pest management, while others concentrate on financial considerations and integrating information across domains. Because the class meets for only 50 minutes and lab sessions are entirely devoted to field trips, teams must coordinate their work independently, which can be challenging for busy students. To help with this, Pritts has begun to offer class time for students to work on their projects, though carving out this time while covering other essential course content often presents a challenge.

At the beginning of the semester, the entire class interviews the partner grower over Zoom to learn about the farm’s basic characteristics, such as its location, soils, existing infrastructure, available labor, water supply, and any family or business constraints. Throughout the semester, students compile follow-up questions as they learn more in class, and the growers’ responses are shared with everyone. This ongoing exchange helps students practice inquiry and professional communication while refining their understanding of the farm’s needs.

Over the course of the semester, each team develops a comprehensive plan for the partner farm, considering the local market, soils, water, existing equipment, potential purchases, and costs. Teams refine their recommendations through ongoing questions and responses with the grower. In the final lab session, the grower comes to campus to hear each team’s 20‑minute pitch.

## Challenges

The greatest challenge for Pritts has been identifying farmers who are early enough in their careers to be flexible and receptive to new ideas. Cooperative Extension educators have been essential partners in this process, helping him locate growers who are just beginning to explore berry production and are eager for guidance.

A second challenge is the distance to participating farms. Thus far, the farms Pritts and the students have worked with have been too far away for students to visit during a 3-hour lab session. It would be ideal for students to visit the partner farm in person before working on their projects, but limiting the project to nearby growers would significantly reduce the pool of participants. Because of this, the class works with growers from across the state and conducts the initial interview with the partner grower over Zoom.

For students, the greatest challenge has consistently been coordinating meeting times to work on their team projects, since most of their collaboration happens outside of class.

Accessibility is also a consideration for this course. The outdoor and field‑based components of the course may require individualized accommodations and alternatives for students with mobility needs.

## Reflection and Future Directions

![image](https://blogs.cornell.edu/teachingcasestudies/files/2026/03/image1-1024x768.jpg)Students learn how to prune raspberries at the Cornell Orchards.

Students benefit from this project on multiple levels. They feel they are contributing to an actual working farm rather than completing a hypothetical exercise, and they learn to apply course concepts to real‑world situations in an integrative, meaningful way. The experience also pushes them to use higher‑level critical thinking by bringing together biological, physical, economic, and human factors when making recommendations—an opportunity few classroom exercises can provide. Teams are intentionally composed so each member can make distinct contributions, giving students meaningful practice with collaborative work.

Pritts also highlights a unique outcome of the project: The experience of receiving genuine gratitude for their efforts. As Pritts notes, the kind of appreciation students receive from their partner grower is different from earning an A and can be a powerful motivator, encouraging students to deepen their learning so they can better support others in the future.

Through their participation, students also learn that farmers rely on a wide network of advisors and support systems—many of which offer potential career paths. In the College of Agriculture and Life Sciences, for example, there are Cornell Cooperative Extension positions for people with master’s degrees to work with growers on a range of production issues. According to Pritts, the project “…kind of puts [students] in the place of the farmer, and they can see a bigger picture about the people they have to work with to be successful in farming.”

Beyond technical knowledge, students also gain insight into family dynamics that shape farm decisions. In one recent collaboration, two young sisters were working through a transition from an older father who preferred to keep control and maintain established practices. Pritts observed that encountering these real family negotiations introduced a social dimension to the course that he “could never have created on [his] own.”

> “Students get a chance to experience gratitude [for their work], which they never, ever get a chance to experience in the classroom… and I know how good that can feel, and how motivating that can be.”
>
> —Marvin Pritts

Farmers consistently enjoy visiting campus, meeting students, and hearing the wide range of ideas that the teams generate—many of which they had never considered. Pritts notes that growers also love and appreciate the “brainstorming dump of ideas,” even though they are under no obligation to act on any of the recommendations. In at least one case, a grower invited students to follow along on social media as their suggested changes were implemented and unfolded.

> “It was so refreshing to hear innovative ideas that were so insightful. They covered every area from bugs to soil to how to increase business and market. Thank you so much for choosing our farm for this project.” – Partner Grower

## How to Adapt This Approach

- **Keep teams small**. Smaller groups make it easier for students to find meeting times and move work forward.
- **Build in coordination time early.** Provide time early in the semester for teams to set roles, expectations, and meeting times.
- **Provide class time for project work.**Because it can be challenging for students to work around conflicting schedules, offering class time for project work can alleviate some of this stress.
- **Leverage connections with community partners.** Draw on existing relationships to help identify partners whose needs match your course objectives for your students.
- **Design the project to deliver tangible value to the partner.**When having students work with an external partner, structure the assignment so that students produce practical recommendations or materials that can realistically be applied to the partner’s work.
