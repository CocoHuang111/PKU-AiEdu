# Students Turn Theory into Practice with Reading Group Guides for Middle School Youth

Over the course of a semester, Cornell students collaborate with middle school teachers and students to create reading group guides, incorporating learning about developmental theory and educational practice.

## **Brief Summary**

Students in Misha Ailsworth’s human development course on Black girlhood collaborated with the eighth grade English language arts teachers and students at DeWitt Middle School, Ithaca City School District, to develop comprehensive reading discussion guides. The guides bridged theory and practice, incorporating historically and culturally relevant pedagogies. Students integrated feedback from teachers and middle schoolers into the guides, and facilitated discussions with the students over two visits during the semester.

## Context

In Misha Ailsworth’s advanced undergraduate and graduate seminar, students examined how history, culture, and power shape experiences and representations of Black girlhood. Through literature, media, and developmental theory, they analyzed identity, representation, and the social conditions that influence Black girls’ lives.

Ailsworth developed the reading group guide assignment two years ago, after meeting with a DeWitt Middle School teacher on the eighth grade English language arts (ELA) team and seeing the potential for college and middle school students to learn from each other through a shared literary experience. The assignment was designed to help her students move from abstract theoretical concepts to practical application and learn how to work with community partners.

> “It’s felt affirming to see that this idea I had a few years ago now has some meat to it and has been successful… it’s pushed me as a pedagogue.” —Misha Ailsworth

The translation from theory to practice could be a challenge for Ailsworth’s students, who initially struggled to create clear, age‑appropriate questions and activities grounded in the scholarship they had studied. The project gave them structured opportunities to practice these skills, receive feedback from teachers and youth, and refine their approach. In doing so, they also examined their assumptions about audience, identity, and knowledge.

## Implementation

***Preparing Students for the Work***

Ailsworth built opportunities for students to practice the analytic and facilitation skills they would need in her class before the reading guide project began. Students studied theoretical frameworks and developmental theory, completed early analytic work (such as a content analysis of Black girlhood in media), and practiced leading discussions in class. They co‑facilitated weekly readings, drafted discussion questions, and received peer feedback as they refined pacing, scaffolding, and how to move a conversation across student responses and to notice how conversations ebb and flow.

***Partnering with Teachers and Middle Schoolers***

![image](https://blogs.cornell.edu/teachingcasestudies/files/2026/03/IMG_6936-2-1024x768.jpg)Professor Ailsworth (far right) with DeWitt Middle School English Language Arts teachers in the school library.

Early in the semester, Ailsworth worked with the DeWitt ELA teachers to develop a shared list of books with Black girl protagonists that both groups of students could choose from. About a third of the way into the semester, Cornell students met with the DeWitt ELA team over Zoom to discuss curriculum design, group dynamics, and how teachers support marginalized students. This conversation grounded the undergraduates in real classroom practice.

Next, teachers at DeWitt invited middle school students to meet with college students to discuss their writing and learn about the books the college students were reading. The teachers organized a visit to Cornell for the students to meet. Undergraduates learned about the students’ reading interests, engaged in icebreakers, and read samples of their writing in order to get to know them better. These interactions helped students tailor their materials to specific learners, rather than to a hypothetical audience.

***Drafting, Testing, and Refining the Reading Discussion Guides***

Cornell students worked together in groups of three and four to develop their reading guides. Each guide included an introduction to the book and author, pre‑discussion and discussion questions, key quotations, and resources to extend conversation.

The Cornell students then brought their draft guides to DeWitt and tried out selected questions and activities in small‑group discussions. Eighth graders provided feedback verbally and through brief notes on index cards. Undergraduates then revised their guides using this feedback, along with suggestions from the teachers, and shared the revised versions with the ELA team for classroom use.

## Challenges

Coordinating the project across the university and the middle school created the biggest challenges. While the Cornell and DeWitt students made strong initial connections over the course of their two meetings, they all wanted more time for real relationship-building, and in particular asked for expanded opportunities to communicate with each other over the books.

Designing age‑appropriate questions and activities was difficult early on. Undergraduates sometimes created prompts that were too abstract or academically framed, and the short timeline made it hard to incorporate all the insights partner teachers shared. Moving forward, Ailsworth is hoping to incorporate more regular communication mechanisms to address this.

> “I’ve been pleasantly surprised by the receptivity of the teachers, and each year, I know that it’s a big undertaking to coordinate when they come to campus… They take a bus and coordinate all sorts of things like permission slips. And every year, they’re always willing to find the time.”
> —Misha Ailsworth

## Reflection

![image](https://blogs.cornell.edu/teachingcasestudies/files/2026/03/Dewitt_activity-501x1024.jpg)Top: DeWitt Middle School, Ithaca City School District. Middle: Written feedback from a middle school student. Bottom: Student at table works on an activity for the book You Truly Assumed by Laila Sabreen.

When Ailsworth first began collaborating with the DeWitt ELA team, the idea that they could get all the students in one place at one time seemed insurmountable, considering schedules, transportation, permission slips, liability issues, etc. She’s been gratified by the receptivity of the teachers at DeWitt, who have taken on the responsibility for coordinating their students’ trip to campus each semester.

What has surprised her most is how quickly the middle school and college students connect. During the first year, an 8th grader asked to hug all the undergraduates goodbye after only an hour‑long discussion — an example of the openness and relationship‑building that has characterized the project. These experiences give younger students a sense of what college could be like for them.

For undergraduates, the assignment has produced a powerful shift in how they understand their roles as learners and knowledge producers. Creating discussion guides for real youth pushes them to move from abstract theory to concrete, age‑appropriate application. Many Cornell students described a change in how they viewed knowledge and expertise—working directly with middle schoolers helped them to see the younger students as expert knowledge producers, not passive recipients. Several Cornell students were surprised by how much they learned from the middle schoolers’ insights, interpretations, and creative responses to the books.

The experience also challenged the Cornell students’ assumptions about what makes a question accessible. After seeing how the eighth graders responded, many realized their original questions were too academic or abstract. This feedback prompted them to rethink how to scaffold questions, communicate clearly, and design activities that supported engagement without oversimplifying the ideas. That process strengthened their skills in crafting culturally responsive and developmentally appropriate activities.

Finally, the Cornell students reported gaining insight into aspects they would not have encountered through readings alone—such as the influence of family dynamics, community context, and lived experiences on how middle schoolers engaged with literature. These interactions helped students better understand the complexity of Black girls’ identities and the importance of designing literacy tools that affirm those realities.

> “I think it’s been successful… every year, my students, in their final reflections, name things like, I didn’t realize how much 8th graders know… and how just working with them has helped me realize they’re smarter than I think, and I’m building my capacity to scaffold.” – Misha Ailsworth

> This assignment was both helpful and challenging, as I had to learn to effectively communicate complex topics. I see this being a useful skill that will translate beyond my academic career.” – Cornell Student

## Future Directions

A consistent theme in the Cornell students’ feedback was the desire for more sustained interaction with the same group of middle schoolers. Many felt that trust and connection were only beginning to form when the interactions ended. Building on this feedback, Ailsworth hopes to extend the project beyond a single-semester experience. Future iterations may include more regular meetings with partner teachers, continued collaboration across academic terms, or a multi‑semester structure in which undergraduates return to work with the same middle schoolers. This expanded model would give both groups the time needed to develop meaningful connections, share feedback, and witness each other’s growth over a longer period.

Looking ahead, Ailsworth plans to expand the reading groups to include students aged 12–18, build in a joint debrief with partner teachers (and, when possible, with middle school students), and maintain the partnership through regular touchpoints and informal relationship‑building, such as coffee chats and casual check‑ins.

> “The development of the reading discussion guide was the most enriching and helpful experience this semester, allowing me to apply theoretical knowledge in a tangible way that not only taught but also inspired and empowered young learners…”
>
> —Cornell Student

## How to Adapt This Approach

- **Sustain relationships intentionally.**Community‑engaged partnerships require regular communication and relational work, not one‑off exchanges. Ailsworth held informal coffee chats, quick after‑school calls, and consistent check‑ins with the DeWitt teachers.
- **Understand and respect school capacity.** K–12 schools are often overextended with people wanting to do research and teaching related to the school, so instructors should be mindful of adding unnecessary burdens on the teachers and school system. Ailsworth took care to acknowledge the teaching team’s workload and keep the project flexible and sensitive to their time constraints.
- **Establish mutual benefit early.** Partnerships should clarify what each group hopes to give and receive. Ailsworth had conversations with the teachers to identify priorities and asked them what they wanted the partnership to look like each semester.
- **Offer material support when possible.** Removing material barriers strengthens engagement. When Ailsworth used research funds to buy the middle schoolers their own books; a teacher described it as feeling “like Christmas.”
- **Be flexible and ready to pivot.** Schools operate within shifting schedules and constraints.
- **Avoid one‑sided exchanges.** Ensure that learning and contribution flow both ways. Ailsworth had college students read and respond to the middle schoolers’ writing, creating reciprocal sharing rather than a top‑down interaction.
- **Document the partnership as it unfolds.** Tracking decisions and challenges strengthens future iterations. Ailsworth suggests keeping clear records from early years and maintaining a partnership journal.
- **Consider long‑term or multi‑semester models.**Sustained engagement supports trust, continuity, and deeper learning.
