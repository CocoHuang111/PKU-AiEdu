Students learned about the complexity and interconnectedness of food systems and public health through class visits and hands-on experiences.

## **Brief Summary**

Students analyzed and contextualized food systems and impacts on public health through class field visits in the Ithaca community and on the Cornell campus. To understand food production, the class visited some of Cornell’s agricultural production facilities, such as the Teaching Dairy Barn and aquaponics facility. To appreciate food system value chains, students shopped at grocery stores using the USDA Healthy Food Basket weekly budget allocations. A volunteer experience to prepare free meals at a community kitchen provided a new perspective on food equity and insecurity. Weekly discussion encouraged students to integrate field experiences with course content, fostering ongoing reflection that extended learning beyond each visit. Overall, these hands-on experiences enabled students to understand theoretical frameworks in concrete contexts, identifying feedback loops, stakeholder power dynamics, and health pathways operating within real food systems.

## Context

Offered since 2017, VTPEH 6121: Food Systems and Health is a course in which students analyze connections between food systems and health by using concepts, theories, and methods from multiple disciplines. To achieve this in the most recent iteration of the course, instructor Kathryn (Katie) Fiorella paired local field trips with pre-readings, lectures introducing relevant frameworks, and in-class discussions and reflective assignments.

After incorporating field trips, Fiorella saw the difference these hands-on experiences had on student learning, noting that students in semesters that have included field trips grasped the topics much better and understood systems more concretely. She also noted that “popular media often shapes students’ assumptions about food systems, and not all of those assumptions hold up. Seeing real production helps recalibrate understanding of dairy, animal agriculture, and other systems.”

> “Popular media often shapes students’ assumptions about food systems, and not all of those assumptions hold up. Seeing real production helps recalibrate understanding of dairy, animal agriculture, and other systems.”—Katie Fiorella

## Implementation

Fiorella implemented a framework to integrate the field trips within the course content. Prior to visiting each site, students completed pre-readings and attended a lecture on a specific topic (e.g., aquatic food systems). They then visited the relevant site (e.g., the recirculating aquaculture system on campus) and upon returning participated in related follow-up discussions, activities, or short presentations.

To understand food production, Fiorella made use of Cornell’s rich agricultural production facilities, including:

- [Cornell Crops of the World Garden](https://blogs.cornell.edu/worldcrops/). Students identified global food crops, some of which they were familiar with, and others that, in many cases, they had never seen.
- [Teaching Dairy Barn](https://cals.cornell.edu/cast-farm-future/about-us/cast-farms/teaching-dairy-barn). Students learned about the high-tech monitoring of dairy production and the range of strategies engaged to keep cows comfortable and reduce carbon emissions.
- Cornell experimental field ([Cornell University Agricultural Experiment Station](https://cals.cornell.edu/agricultural-experiment-station)). Students gleaned tomatoes for donation to the [Friendship Donations Network](https://friendshipdonations.org/), an Ithaca-based food rescue organization.
- Aquaponics facility ([Cornell Cooperative Extension – Aquaculture](https://blogs.cornell.edu/aquaculture/research/)). Students experienced a cutting-edge technological solution to increase the production of fish and vegetables and reduce nutrient pollution.
- Grocery stores. Students in small groups shopped using the USDA Healthy Food Basket weekly budget allocations.
- [Loaves & Fishes of Tompkins County](https://loaves.org/). Students volunteered at this community kitchen that provides free meals, hospitality, and companionship.

> “Being there in person and thinking about shopping with a particular budget gives you a lens into what it might be like to actually be in that position.”—Katie Fiorella

Finally, students celebrated food culture by sharing something they cooked or brought and its importance to their own food culture.

Class sessions following field trips focused on discussion, where students connected their field observations to course content. These discussions created an ongoing space for students to weave together experiential insights and course readings, extending learning beyond the field trips. The repetitive structure of learning about the place they were visiting, going out to experience it firsthand, discussing it upon returning to class, and then applying those insights to course assignments and projects helped the students build a stronger and more practical understanding of how food systems work.

## Challenges

The main challenges for incorporating field site visits into this course were logistical. Those challenges included obtaining or otherwise ensuring student access to transportation for the field trips; making sure that students traveling independently could find the location; ensuring students had appropriate gear for the variety of site visits; and unpredictable weather.

To mitigate these, Fiorella:

- Planned site visits on campus or within walking/busing distance. This helped to overcome transportation barriers and avoid the costs of renting fleet vehicles. Students were also able to drive their personal vehicles if preferred, with appropriate waivers completed at the beginning of class.
- Planned outdoor excursions for early fall to help to avoid weather disruptions as much as possible.
- Moved her class time to the end of the day. This relieved the pressure of getting students back to campus in time for other classes.
- Ensured clear and consistent communication. This was essential—Fiorella gave frequent reminders about dressing warmly and choosing appropriate footwear for the activity and made her phone number available to students to use if they got lost.

## Reflection and Future Directions

![image](https://blogs.cornell.edu/teachingcasestudies/files/2026/03/hands_on-916x1024.jpg)Students participate in course activities at Dilmun Hill Student Farm.

While all of the field trips contributed to the students’ learning, Fiorella observed that the students gained more from the field trips in which they were able to participate in an activity, as opposed to those where their primary role was to listen and observe. Examples of hands-on activities that impacted students included gleaning tomatoes for donation and going grocery shopping using USDA Healthy Food Basket weekly budget allocations. According to Fiorella, “being there in person and thinking about shopping with a particular budget gives you a lens into what it might be like to actually be in that position.”

Fiorella also noted that she’s had to make changes to class activities that she previously implemented following the field trips, as the written reflections she used to assign no longer feel practical in a post-AI classroom. The present iteration of the course prioritizes class discussions and presentations instead.

> “Students better understand challenges when they’ve had hands-on exposure—like harvesting tomatoes—even briefly. It reshapes their assumptions about why certain industry practices exist.”—Katie Fiorella

## How to Adapt This Approach

With these reflections and the logistical challenges in mind, Fiorella recommends the following:

- Start with just one or two near-campus experiences that align with the course content.
- Explore field trip options that allow students to be directly involved in a process or take part in a hands-on activity, rather than just listening.
- Precede the field trip with the applicable course content so that students are aware of what they should observe or look for when on the excursion (e.g., discuss nutrition and carbon emissions from dairy production before visiting the Teaching Dairy Barn). Fiorella notes that “students remember the things we talk about in class differently because they see the connection in the same week.”
- Provide opportunities for reflection and discussion to cement learning. This can be done in a variety of ways (e.g., taking a photograph, small group discussion, presentations, oral reflections, etc).
- For assessments, consider integrating the field trips into assignments or exams, asking questions about what students learned on the trips to ensure they recognize them as essential for learning rather than optional, add-on excursions.
