September 21, 2026 | AI Meets Education at Stanford

## Lessons from the 2026 Course and Curriculum Seed Grant Proposals

Cassandra Volpe Horii
Associate Vice Provost for Education and Director of the Center for Teaching and Learning

In April 2026, Stanford University announced [$1 million in seed grants](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2026/04/seed-grants-ai-education) to catalyze innovation in courses and curricula, research, and critical thinking, all related to AI, teaching, and learning in higher education. The “Course and Curriculum” seed grant track, one of three in the suite of opportunities, received over 60 proposals from instructors across the university and awarded [12 grants](https://ctl.stanford.edu/news/ai-education-grants-2026) in summer 2026. The grants focus on changes in 25 course offerings over the upcoming academic year, across four schools and units, with an estimated total enrollment of approximately 3700, including both undergraduate and graduate students.

The grantees’ projects include an impressive array of approaches to teaching in this unique moment, adapting to the rapid growth of generative and agentic AI tools. The wider field of proposals also contains important lessons, revealing a snapshot of Stanford faculty and lecturers’ thinking about education and AI today. The [AI Meets Education at Stanford (AIMES)](https://ctl.stanford.edu/aimes) initiative is sharing this synthesis of challenges, potential solutions, and emerging directions to help spark further innovation.

### AI and Education Challenges and Opportunities

Among the challenges and opportunities that Stanford instructors are seeking to address, the 2026 Course and Curriculum Seed Grants process demonstrates a strong focus on productive struggle. A paramount concern among instructors is that undergraduate and graduate students may use AI tools to avoid the uncomfortable—but essential—stages of learning. But instructors too are grappling with the changing nature of knowledge, boundaries between human work and that of AI, methods of discipline-based discovery, and how to best teach in this shifting landscape. They frequently acknowledge the unknowns of the present and ask what it means to be AI-literate and fluent, now and in the future. Those engaged with AI and education are, collectively, in a state of productive struggle, while seeking to maintain and enhance such states with their students.

Ethical and value-driven concerns also appear frequently, spanning the social, civic, and environmental dimensions of AI. These issues include debates about the value of and impacts on humans and their work in the context of growing uses of AI; about the governance, control, use, and economics of new technologies; and about energy and community implications of data centers needed for the expanding generative AI industry. Instructors are working to raise and discuss ethical challenges with students while engaging them in new technologies, with heightened critical appraisal and awareness of the ethical stakes. Some faculty are considering how to embed ethical analysis of AI’s implications across curricula in ways that align with professional standards and expectations. Human-centered approaches are prevalent across fields, consistent with several of the principles put forth in the [AI at Stanford Advisory Committee’s 2025 report](https://provost.stanford.edu/news/report-ai-stanford-advisory-committee): Human Oversight, Human Alignment, and Human Professionalism.

We expected a focus on academic integrity, and indeed it is present. So is career preparation, to varying degrees. But these anticipated concerns do not appear in isolation. Rather, instructors tend to frame one main challenge as a nexus connecting multiple related issues. For example, projects focusing on changing assessment strategies also elevate person-to-person interaction that facilitates critical dialogue and provides practice for valuable career skills. Proposed simulations bring career-relevant preparation into class, and open new possibilities for authentic and verifiable assessment strategies.

### AI and Education Solutions

After reading all proposals multiple times and making notes about their themes, informed by the Center for Teaching and Learning’s extensive consultations with students and instructors on AI and education, I worked with Claude* to try out various ways of clustering and analyzing them. Claude reported an interesting disciplinary difference: “build something” in engineering and medicine; “preserve something” in the humanities and writing-based fields.

That sounds catchy, but it’s incorrect. In fact, across disciplines, Stanford instructors are working on preserving the depth of critical inquiry and the ability to bring human ingenuity to new questions and problems in their fields—hallmarks of a research university environment like ours. Similarly, across fields, the 2026 course and curriculum seed grant proposals showcase the need for and engagement with building new approaches (some using AI tools, some not). Preserving and building go hand-in-hand.

A multi-course project in Art and Art History is an interesting example of preserving and building, where course redesign efforts involve both leaning into AI use to discover failures and biases that further inform creative work, and grounding AI use in slowness, course community interaction, and physical and material making. These approaches preserve core disciplinary ways of making and knowing while building new pedagogies and methods that engage with AI.

Numerous approaches to fostering productive struggle and protecting assessment integrity are already being piloted, with more to come. They tackle motivation, student confidence, curricular coherence, reading engagement, and metacognition, and they emphasize the process of learning and making, rather than only the end result. They also focus on clear, shared norms that are part of the ongoing conversation between students and instructors, not just a line in the syllabus (though clear policies in the syllabus are also essential). For example, a Program in Writing and Rhetoric project will explore the co-creation of AI use class norms. Changes to assessment go well beyond proctored and oral exams, too. Proposals included multiple examples of focusing on the unique artifacts of students’ processes of learning and developing their ideas that can be assessed. Analog pedagogies, as in the COLLEGE project focusing on in-depth reading and the growing pressure to use AI-generated summaries in place of close reading, play important roles, alongside various kinds of experiential learning (e.g., field experiences, making prototypes and projects for real-world use).

Three Computer Science projects aim to approach productive struggle and assessment integrity—especially motivating and verifying student learning—in different ways. They each propose to restructure teaching time to include more small group tutorials (in-depth discussions with a few students and one TA) and one-to-one coding interviews (students explaining their approaches and reasoning, with individualized feedback), with AI supporting portions of the process of scaling those approaches to large classes of 150–300 students.

Potential solutions to developing students’ professional readiness integrate sound AI decision making into courses: when, how, and why to use AI, to create with AI, to manage with AI, to troubleshoot AI, and to collaborate with AI. As agentic capabilities become more readily available, the need for students’ development of facility with these tools is also on the rise—learning to deploy and control them, and using agents to better approach and assess their own learning (e.g., via self-made simulations of course concepts), with a heightened need for caution and critique.

### Emerging Directions

The 2026 seed grant process also pointed to several emerging sets of questions and directions. Stanford instructors are noticing ways in which AI enables students to study further afield of their majors and extend disciplinary work to include new methods and approaches. Vibe coding and new digital techniques in the arts and humanities are key examples of this trend. From an educational point of view, students now have access to capabilities for which they may not (yet) have acquired the deep technical background to be able to evaluate the outputs of their work. Instructors are wrestling with questions like how to teach the methods and the evaluative skills at the same time, and whether to embed this exploration within courses on other subjects or focus entire courses on AI-driven methods.

Rethinking the relationship between practice and demonstration of learning is another burgeoning field of change. Learning still requires practice and feedback, but AI tools have brought into question who or what is doing the practicing and getting the feedback, and how to both define what is most essential for students to learn to do on their own and create structures that motivate human work. Adding to this challenge, in some areas, instructors have sufficient certainty about the essentially human aspects of thinking, learning, and creating to guide students, and in others, the nature of future human work is very much in debate. Within this environment, Stanford faculty and lecturers are rethinking the “stakes” of different forms of student work, where and when they are produced, how and in what form feedback is shared and discussed, and what artifacts are most meaningfully evaluated to observe learning.

As the 2026-2027 Course and Curriculum Seed Grant projects progress, and as instructors consider building on their pilots and experiments to propose new projects for 2027–28, all of us working on the AIMES initiative hope that these themes inform and inspire your teaching and learning.
