## AI Meets Education at Stanford Reflections, AY ’25-‘26

Michele Elam
Senior Associate Vice Provost in the Office of the Vice Provost for Undergraduate Education; William Robertson Coe Professor in the Humanities; Professor of English; and Senior Fellow, Institute for Human-Centered AI (HAI)

### AI and Educational Challenges

The impact of generative AI in the last year, especially on the campus climate, has been extensive, dynamic, and at times, verging on the divisive. Touching almost all aspects of the educational experience in and outside the class, AI has been the subject of intense discussion and debate among students, staff and faculty (not to mention the wider university community); as I hope to suggest, Stanford’s responses to AI’s growing presence within teaching, learning, research, advising, and administration reflect national trends while also bearing the imprint of the university's distinctive culture and structure.

My AI Meets Education at Stanford (AIMES) reflections of this last year are informed by dozens of formal and informal meetings with department and program chairs, directors of undergraduate study, academic advisors, student services coordinators for both undergraduate and graduate students, student affinity groups, my own classes, and countless conversations with colleagues across the fields of STEM, social science, humanities, and arts. They also reflect my ongoing service on several university committees on AI, my professional research in the areas of AI ethics and policy at HAI, meetings with colleagues at other universities and colleges, attendance at conferences on AI and education, and are influenced by CTL’s development of AIMES under Cassandra Horii’s leadership.

My reflections from the past year offer takeaways in three areas: campus climate, policy and guidance, and AI literacies.

#### 1. Campus Climate

I highlight three features of Stanford’s campus climate that are especially relevant to AI:

#### Language (re)shaping campus culture

For many years, Stanford has been burdened by a “techie” versus “fuzzy” divide: the former usually refers to people in computing and quantitative disciplines; and the latter to those in the humanities and other qualitative fields. Both terms come with negative connotations. The casual and more recent adoption across campus of terminology referring to “early adopters” or “neo-Luddites,” and of “boomers,” “doomers” or “resistors,” all relative to AI, replicates and amplifies many of the same limiting associations with “techie” and “fuzzy,” implying that some people are ahead of the curve, visionary, forward-thinking, the world’s doers and makers, while others are backward, navel-gazing, at best orbital, ornamental, or at worst obstructive to civilizational progress. The impact of these connotations has shown up perhaps most vividly in my students’ conversations about their choices of majors and their interpretation (rightly or wrongly and almost always without evidence) of their educational choices’ respective cultural, educational, and professional importance. Yet many students, faculty, and administrators have acknowledged that their actual experiences often didn’t fit these categories, neither in principle nor practice. Most spoke of how their research and educational experiences, in fact, cross disciplinary divides; many faculty, even those engaged in basic research, regularly bridge domains of expertise and spoke of how much they appreciated that the interdisciplinary liberal arts mission at Stanford encouraged and enabled this.

A takeaway is that Stanford should not be sanguine about the circulation of these terms: they are not innocuous or harmless. Rather, they risk unnecessarily dividing campus communities and constituencies; they mistakenly message both internally and externally that Stanford prizes technology and STEM above all intellectual endeavors; and they strain efforts to support the university’s broader educational mission and vibrant community life. Reframing the language around AI away from the apocalyptic or salvific, away from labeling that has the effects of diminishing or disenfranchising, enables more nuanced discussions about AI design, development, and deployment. It will also help support a positive campus climate and shared community norms around these issues.

#### Acceleration vs “AI Pacing”

Many across campus, even those that adopt Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg’s mantra of “move fast and break things,” expressed being overwhelmed, if not explicitly resentful, with the constant emphasis on acceleration and speed associated with AI. Over the past year, I heard students and colleagues across campus express frustration that the start-up ideals of velocity and “disruption”—a term [Jill Lepore](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2014/06/23/the-disruption-machine) has notably debunked—seem to be imposed on university work and life without consent. This issue is perhaps the most divisive on campus that I have noticed: some argue that acceleration is either a technological inexorability, an educational necessity, or a national security need. Others argue—especially when it comes to education—that critical reflection, caution, analysis, longitudinal educational studies, red-teaming, and risk assessment of social, environmental, educational, and existential harms more than warrant at least some slowed pacing to AI deployment in schools. Most I spoke with, probably because we are embedded in Silicon Valley, expressed a sense that the rhetoric of AI acceleration had won the day (at least as of the writing of this essay) and, depending on one’s perspective, felt validated or disheartened by it.

A takeaway: The pedal-to-the-floor approach to AI in education, while a driving, even messianic, mission for some, is also damaging morale for others. There are certainly ways to mitigate this tension at the institutional level and some are underway, such as meaningful conversations through AIMES, HAI, the Stanford Accelerator, and elsewhere on campus. It is worth pursuing these and other mitigations, as the pace of AI is the source of much friction, particularly among faculty and across disciplines.

#### Shifting Attitudes about Educational Value and Purpose

Amid federal skepticism about higher education, coupled with students’ anxiety about economy instability and AI’s impact on work, undergraduates’ are reassessing the value and purposes of a Stanford degree. Despite widespread resistance to the notion of vocational education as an end in itself, and to the recent government argument that educational value ought to be measured by income level within the first couple years of graduation, many Stanford students still hold to what W.E.B. Du Bois understood as the core civic and spiritual functions of higher education, that “The true college will ever have one goal—not to earn meat, but to know the end and aim of that life which meat nourishes.”

A takeaway: Stanford can make a stronger case, internally and externally, for the lifelong value of higher education in personal, civic, and professional life. Doing so is a shared responsibility across the university.

When I recently invited Kevin Scott, CTO of Microsoft, to speak to my class on AI, he emphasized how he preferred hiring people who had liberal arts backgrounds, even over those with specific technical preparation in AI (as he put it, “we can train people in the tech we use; but we can’t teach them how to think”). There were audible sighs of relief from many in the class, including from CS majors who knew coding skills alone were no longer a direct career onramp. A student in class, who identified as generationally low-income, said to me, Scott’s words gave her “license to embrace the fields of study I was actually most passionate about,” trusting she would be “better prepared for whatever multiple careers” she ended up in over her lifetime. While we don’t need industry titans to make the case, it matters to students to hear that message from diverse quarters. As a result, in addition to domain experts, I usually invite speakers to class from industry, government, and civil society, as well as alumni to offer “real-world” views about both the practical-economic implications and higher-order values of higher education (lest they doubt their humanities professor on this subject).

### 2. Policy and Guidance

One refrain I have heard consistently and more intensely over time is a shared desire for AI policy guidance and clarity. Stanford has not advocated for nor adopted a university-wide AI policy, as many would prefer approaches that preserve faculty discretion over provost-led governance. That said, there are definitely calls for greater norming, guidance, oversight, and information.

Conversations with faculty and students consistently revealed concerns focusing on academic integrity, the Honor Code, and AI-enabled cheating, but quickly extended beyond them. Indeed, the issue of cheating proved to be a thread pulled to reveal a whole tapestry of confusion and consternation around AI use in educational contexts. Specifically:

Many were unfamiliar with debates over the use of various AI detection services (e.g., the lawsuits over violations of privacy, unauthorized data collection, documented bias, and inaccuracies) and about university judicial processes handling accusations of AI cheating. Related, most were unaware of—or had not considered the implications of—the use of third-party commercial AI products in their classroom assignments. The few that heard about the recent Stanford licensing of some products were unsure how to receive them or reluctant to give up the “personalization” of those they had been using. They did not know where efforts existed across campus to address cheating and often did not view the issue in a broader educational context and wider pedagogical responses, ongoing both at Stanford and nationally, as an occasion to revisit teaching and learning best practices.

The Academic Integrity Working Group, with CTL, spoke to the chairs and deans in fall 2025, and about two dozen departments subsequently arranged custom workshops throughout the academic year through AIMES, so knowledge of resources grew over the year. However, many faculty and students remained unaware of resources like AIMES, such as the [library of teaching examples](https://ctl.stanford.edu/aimes/aimes-library-examples) or the [Critical AI Literacy for Instructors](https://ctl.stanford.edu/aimes/ai-literacy-instructors-canvas) self-paced course, and confessed that they had little time to explore them once they were informed.

During the 2025–26 academic year, the more than 300 faculty and lecturers across 25+ departments and programs who attended AIMES workshops designed for their disciplines gained understanding of how they could develop their own AI syllabus policies, adapt assignments, and collaborate on their approaches. But there remains wide divergence between policies both between faculty within departments and across them. Some departments have developed department-wide policies, while in others faculty develop their own. Policies developed at these local levels vary in how informed they are by University guidance, such as that offered by the [AI at Stanford Advisory Committee](https://provost.stanford.edu/news/report-ai-stanford-advisory-committee) (on which I serve), [University IT](https://uit.stanford.edu/security/responsibleai) guidance, [Honor Code](https://communitystandards.stanford.edu/policies-guidance%23policies-guidance-links/bca-guidance-recommendations#generative-ai-policy-guidance) guidance (which has been vague, with revisions in the works for 2026–27), legal issues involving student privacy and copyright, and relevant scholarly research.

They also were unaware of resources like the AI Tinkery, nor how to tap into the research happening in centers of AI expertise like the [Institute for Human Centered AI](https://hai.stanford.edu/) (HAI) or the [Stanford Accelerator for Learning](https://acceleratelearning.stanford.edu/). Many wanted to know how to access evidence-based research on the pros or cons of using particular AI systems for teaching, learning and research, having perhaps heard about “cognitive offloading,” but few had read the MIT, Stanford, or EU studies on these topics and did not know where to get, let alone interpret the significance of, the latest emerging research.

A takeaway: These are common communication challenges in a complex and information-rich environment like Stanford, but the pace of AI change exacerbates them. There are many sites on campus that point to this or that university guideline about AI use, but they exist as an archipelago; they are not consolidated nor do they consistently cross-reference each other. Some I discovered are entirely unaware of the others. [AIMES](http://aimes.stanford.edu/) consolidates resources related specifically to teaching and learning related to AI. But currently there does not exist a one-stop shop for all relevant university guidance as it is released nor, importantly, a comprehensive, higher education focused registry of relevant and emerging vetted scholarly research (the Stanford Graduate School of Education [SCALE AI Research Repository](https://scale.stanford.edu/ai/repository) includes higher education, though with a stronger focus on K-12). The GSE is a living repository for some of it, and HAI’s policy briefs and multidisciplinary research significantly expands that and are often represented in useful non-technical terms accessible to the average citizen. But the challenge remains: how can faculty staff and students become more aware of and informed about AI in the context of higher education, and access resources—as they wish—to navigate AI effectively in their classrooms and teaching, recognizing their own limited time, individual commitments, and priorities?

### 3. AI Literacies

My final reflection concerns a shift over the past few years in how we understand AI literacy: away from a narrow focus on technical skills for career preparation and towards what scholars in the United States and Europe call “cultural literacies” or “critical AI literacies”—the ability to engage with AI’s ethical and social implications. Arguably, these capacities are equally, if not more, important for career preparation, and perhaps even more importantly, for preparation as informed citizens. A few years ago, engineering and product-design students and instructors often embraced the maxim, “If you can build it, you should.” Increasingly, however, I hear its reversal: “Just because you can build something doesn’t mean you should”—a sentiment associated with Einstein’s grave concerns about the atomic bomb and memorably voiced by Dr. Ian Malcolm in *Jurassic Park*: “Your scientists were so preoccupied with whether or not they could, they didn’t stop to think if they should.”

Ethics is no longer a belated concern in many classrooms engaging with AI, and “ethics” extends far beyond risk mitigation or industry “audits” to include broader interdisciplinary and socio-technical approaches that take up AI as both a tool and a topic of critical inquiry essential for an informed citizenry. The emergence of campus wide affinity and advocacy groups and spontaneous faculty associations across disciplines on these topics reflects an organic ground-up swelling of interest. Many of us have also begun scaling classes on this expanded concept of AI literacy, offering accompanying public-facing speaker series to reach more of our student body at all stages of their education as well as community members (including staff who often only receive technical training in the workplace).

A takeaway: I see nothing but positives in this move, and it is increasingly reflected in students’ calls to see their training and courses reflect more than technical know-how and to more substantially and critically engage with these issues rather than see them as belated or tangential to their education. Stanford is well-positioned to lead these discussions about what it means to be AI “literate” in a democracy.

I see “critical AI literacies” and “cultural literacies” as part of a broader society-wide approach to AI–one emerging at Stanford and beyond–that recognizes that exponentially powerful and socially impactful technologies warrant everyone’s involvement and insight. The recent [$1 million in seed grants](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2026/04/seed-grants-ai-education), including the [AI Thought Leadership competition](https://ctl.stanford.edu/aimes/seed-grants/thought-leadership-open), is an important effort towards surfacing and supporting diverse voices and innovative ideas across campus, and to giving a broader swath of the Stanford community an opportunity to shape the conversations about, and direction of AI, on campus and potentially beyond.
