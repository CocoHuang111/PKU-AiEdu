# PACE Clear AI Policies

While course AI policies will vary, all courses should provide students with clear guidance on permitted AI use and should utilize AI-resilient assessments to ensure an accurate picture of student mastery.

## How to make it work

- **Include a specific AI use policy in your syllabus**, with clear examples connected to the assignments of your particular course, and a description of how your AI policy supports the course’s learning objectives. For more detailed considerations for implementing AI-prohibited, AI-limited, and AI-assigned policies, see [AIMES (AI Meets Education at Stanford)](https://ctl.stanford.edu/aimes/aimes-library-examples).
- When there are multiple offerings or sections of a given course, there should be **a single AI use policy applied consistently.**
- **Don’t assume a shared set of beliefs about what constitutes ethical or unethical use of AI.** Students frequently differ from instructors in their understanding of how AI use intersects with academic integrity, so it’s essential to communicate expectations clearly.
- Where AI use is permitted, **prompt students to reflect on how and why they are using AI**.
- **Focus on assessment design and in-person validation over detection.** Reliably identifying unpermitted use of AI is effectively impossible. Shift as many assessments as possible to in-person exercises, whether high-stakes, such as [proctored midterms or finals](https://pace.stanford.edu/node/131), or low-stakes, such as regular [understanding checks](https://pace.stanford.edu/node/121).
- **Do not use third-party AI-detection programs**. Not only are such programs unreliable, often flagging text by English language learners incorrectly, but using them can put student data and privacy at risk.

## Syllabus statement template

**Generative AI Policy:** Depending on how you use it, GenAI has the potential to support your learning or rob you of the productive friction that is key to truly mastering the material in this course. It’s important, therefore, that you know exactly what kinds of activities you may use AI for and which you may not.

In this course, you **may** use AI to do the following [*add or remove items as appropriate for your course*]:

- **Tutor yourself** on concepts you’re having trouble with.
- **Quiz yourself** on course material.
- **Brainstorm ideas** for projects or papers.
- **Outline arguments.**
- **Generate feedback** on ideas, problem-solving processes, code, arguments, or writing.
- **Draft timelines and division of labor** for group projects.
- **Locate sources** for projects or papers.
- **Summarize sources** to help you decide whether they’re relevant or not.
- **Copy-edit your writing** for spelling and grammar.
- **Generate graphics** for projects or papers.

In this course, you **may not** use AI to do the following [*add or remove items as appropriate for your course*]:

- **Complete problem-sets.**
- **Draft a complete essay.**
- **Tutor yourself** on concepts you’re having trouble with.
- **Quiz yourself** on course material.
- **Brainstorm ideas** for projects or papers.
- **Outline arguments.**
- **Generate feedback** on ideas, problem-solving processes, code, arguments, or writing.
- **Draft timelines and division of labor** for group projects.
- **Locate sources** for projects or papers.
- **Summarize sources** to help you decide whether they’re relevant or not.
- **Copy-edit your writing** for spelling and grammar.
- **Generate graphics** for projects or papers.

When using AI, be sure to use [Stanford-approved tools](https://aiplayground-prod2.stanford.edu/login). Don’t upload sensitive information or copyrighted material into third-party tools or personal accounts: these haven’t been vetted for data security. In keeping with the standards of academic journals and many professional organizations, any use of AI—including a description of the use as well as the model and prompts used—must be disclosed. Above all, remain a deliberate tool-user and take responsibility for the accuracy of any information: LLMs frequently hallucinate content, quotations, and references, and should not be accepted uncritically. If you have any questions about a particular use of AI, please reach out!

## Real examples from Stanford classes

### From AMSTUD 155S/INTLREL 115/POLISCI 115/PUBPOL 114 with Amy Zegart

**Course AI Policy**
Students who never use AI are missing the chance to harness the power of this technology to work faster and better. Students who rely too much on AI, or use it poorly, will fail to develop the core thinking and writing skills they will need in life.

1. **You must indicate whether and how you have used AI in your memo research**. Do this by putting an AI statement at the top your memo. “I used (specify the AI chatbot) to find sources for this memo.” If TAs find that you used AI for more than what you put in your AI statement, that’s an Honor Code violation.
2. **You are allowed to use AI to find sources for your memo**. But note: AI often makes sources up! TAs will check every source; if any are “hallucinated” by AI, that’s an Honor Code violation—because you are indicating you read a source as an input to your memo when in fact you did not. Because it doesn’t exist. This is research dishonesty.
3. **You are allowed to use AI to summarize sources as a first cut**. But you should then read those materials yourself!
4. **You are NOT allowed to use AI to write any portions of your memo**. The teaching team is feeding prompts and sources into AI, too, so we have a good idea of the typical essays that different AI tools produce. Memos that appear to be AI-generated or that appear to have significant AI-generated portions will trigger an Honor Code investigation. To incentivize you to do the hard work of thinking and writing your memo rather than handing the assignment to GenAI, you will be questioned by your TA about your memo, arguments, and sources and how you came to the analytic judgments and recommendations you wrote.

### From BIO 146 with Jamie Imam

For the work in this class, much of the learning comes from you yourself engaging in the activities and practicing the skills needed, such as reading research papers and other sources, critically analyzing various pieces of evidence, and employing and reflecting upon different strategies for communication of complex ideas. Using generative AI to complete your assignments would take away much of the benefit you would derive from engaging in these important steps yourself (in addition to ethical and accuracy concerns). Therefore, the **use of generative AI to “substantially complete”** an assignment by entering a prompt and submitting the output (or a version of the output) as one’s own work **is not permitted**. There may be some beneficial uses, which we will explore together. However, we require that you **document and share any uses** upon assignment submission. Here are a couple examples of permitted and prohibited uses in this class (see us with any questions):

**Prohibited**

- Generating figures or graphics that make up a large portion of your project
- Prompting a chatbot to write the text for your projects or reflections
- Generating a summary of papers or course readings rather than reading and critically thinking about the findings, conclusions, etc. yourself

**Permitted (with proper documentation of use and your final oversight)**

- Requesting feedback on sentence structure or the tone of portions of your writing (while remaining responsible for evaluating the feedback and making the final decisions)
- Asking for more information on your audience’s perspective to hone your project’s focus
- Asking follow-up questions to clarify or refine your understanding of important terms or biological concepts related to our diseases of study (i.e. serving a tutor function)

**You remain accountable for the content of the work you submit**.

### From HUMBIO 57/EPI 247 with Julie Parsonnet

Because Artificial Intelligence (AI) is becoming ubiquitous in the workplace, we do permit use of AI in this class. There are, however, subtleties in field epidemiology that are not currently identified by AI. If you use AI blindly to answer problems or questions for you, you are likely to miss these subtleties. (Note also that you will not have access to AI for the final examination in this course, so you will be infinitely better off learning the material yourself.)

Please credit AI when used: company and tool/model name (with version or date) and link to specific output if available; prompt used and description of purpose(s) (e.g., brainstorming, outlining, generating images, refining grammar, data processing, etc.).

### From PWR 1 with Lisa Swan

PWR is a class that strives to help you develop as a reader, researcher, and writer. These literacies enable humans to understand ourselves, expand human thought, see different perspectives, empathize with others, and participate in democracy—all important outcomes of any education. Therefore, as a class, we will collectively agree to actively read and write as humans. As your instructor, I will develop the assignments, lesson plans, and course materials independently. I will also write feedback and grade your written work without the assistance of GenAI or LLMs. As a student, I ask that you bring your whole (wonderfully imperfect) human self to our work together. Do not offload your learning to technology for the sake of perceived ease and efficiency. You must actively read your sources and write your language without assistance from GenAI or LLMs. You are not permitted to use or consult language generated by a LLM in any of your work (see PWR GenAI policy). You are permitted to use LLMs in limited ways in the process of exploring your research topic. In class, I will share a chart to help you navigate permitted and not permitted LLM uses. Our work in this class will be challenging, but I know you are capable of meeting this challenge. I am excited to be a human learning alongside you.

## What the research says

- Research on the negative effects of over-reliance on AI for cognitive offloading suggests **inappropriate use of AI measurably decreases memory** (Kosmyna et al., 2025).
- **While it has the potential to enhance learning**, AI can also allow students to bypass the productive struggle that is often essential to mastering new skills (Jose et al., 2025).
- Many students (and instructors) remain unaware of how AI functions and how **it can introduce fabricated sources or results and reproduce or exacerbate existing biases** (Jordan & Tsai, 2022; Popenici, 2025; Jaźwińska & Chandrasekar, 2025).
- As GenAI magnifies existing weaknesses in pedagogy and reduces barriers for students tempted to shortcut their learning processes, **many assessments that previously measured student learning no longer fit the purpose** (Gallant & Rettinger, 2025; Petrocelli, 2025).
- AI detection software may **introduce biases against English language learners** (Liang et al., 2023).
